# Deep Research — Thorough Research Before Writing

You produce content whose facts must be **verified, not recalled from memory**. Mandatory sequence: research first, plan second, write last. A figure, a date, a name or a result enters the content only because you saw it in a source you retrieved or in material provided by the user — and you can say which.

## When to use

- The subject depends on current or verifiable external facts: recent events, market or policy data, product/version specifics, scientific advances, named cases.
- The content taught or published engages the credibility of its author.

## When not to use

- Stable, timeless knowledge (mathematical derivation, classic text, established skill): say so in one sentence and produce the content normally. Do not do "theater research" on a subject that does not need it.

## Method

### Step 1 — Start from what the session already has

List the materials provided by the user (documents, links, data, recordings) **before** any web research. The user's materials are the primary authority on their own subject; web research complements them, it does not replace them. URLs pasted in the conversation can be retrieved directly.

### Step 2 — Break the subject into facets

Break the request into 2–4 queryable facets — distinct questions the content must answer with evidence. Typical facets: current state / latest developments; reference figures and authoritative data; concrete cases and incidents; risks, controversies or open questions. Write the list of facets before searching. A facet of stable knowledge is marked "no research needed" and taught as such.

### Step 3 — Search with a budget

- **Maximum 8 web searches for the whole session**, spread across facets. One precise query is worth more than several vague ones.
- Read each result before searching again: the next query is shaped by what the last one returned, not by a rephrasing.
- Research is part of the work, not the main act. If the session drags, cut facets — never the production.

### Step 4 — Choose the sources, retrieve them

- **Maximum 6 URLs in total** across all facets. Prefer primary and authoritative origins — official bodies, named institutions, the report or dataset itself — and for time-sensitive claims, the most recent. Ignore mirrors and aggregators that repackage the same story.
- Only retrieve URLs that appeared in the user's messages or in the session's search results. **Never fabricate a URL from memory**: if the wanted source did not appear, refine the search rather than guess an address.

### Step 5 — Read in depth, keep the register

- Read each retrieved source far enough to verify every claim you plan to draw from it. Use in-document search to locate a figure or a name rather than re-reading blindly.
- Keep a **claim → source register** (URL or material identifier, plus source name and publication date when visible). Only registered claims may enter the content as researched facts. Note the date: an outdated figure presented as current is a factual error, not a style choice.

### Step 6 — Cross-check conflicts

- Prefer primary over secondary, recent over old for time-sensitive claims, domain authority over generalist media. Two independent origins are worth more than a story republished ten times.
- If a conflict survives — contested figures, divergent official accounts — present the range or the disagreement with both attributions. Do not pick a side in silence, and never invent an average between two contradictory numbers.
- A claim carrying weight and resting on a single source stays single-source: soften the tone, attribute explicitly, or drop it.

### Step 7 — Know when research is done

Research is done when both conditions hold:

- each facet the plan relies on has at least one registered source, or is marked "stable knowledge, no source";
- each number, date and name the content will state is in the register.

Then stop. Polishing research after coverage is reached steals budget from production.

### Step 8 — Anchor the plan

Plan the content in the conversation, then produce it section by section with an explicit brief per section. Write the research into the briefs: the facets, the registered claims with their attribution (source + date), the conflicts and their resolution, the gaps deliberately left open. Structure the content around the researched questions — what was found, what changed, what is contested — not around generic chapter titles.

### Step 9 — Anchor each section

- When you produce a section, pass it the sourced facts of its section — cited concretely (figures, names, results) with their attribution.
- Inside the content, cite naturally — the institution, the report, the year. Do not dump raw URLs on the reader.
- Quizzes, exercises and scenarios also rest on registered facts: a quiz that tests a figure nobody verified teaches noise.

## Hard rules

- **Never invent sources, citations, URLs or publication dates.** A claim without a register entry is presented as stable knowledge with an honest tone, or is not taught.
- If web research is unavailable or keeps failing: say so, build from the user's materials and stable knowledge, and clearly mark what could not be verified. Never present memory as research.
- Time-sensitive claims without a source do not enter the content. Timeless knowledge does not need a source — do not spend budget verifying what an established manual already states.
- In case of conflict between user material and web research: on the facts of the user's own subject (their data, their product, their case), their material wins. On external context, the best-verified recent source wins — and you flag the disagreement to the user instead of silently overloading.

## Verification before finishing

- [ ] Each covered facet has a registered source or is marked "stable".
- [ ] Each figure, date, name in the final content is in the register.
- [ ] Conflicts are presented with both attributions, or resolved explicitly.
- [ ] No URL fabricated from memory.
- [ ] What could not be verified is marked as such.
