# Feynman Learning — Learning by Explaining (Feynman method)

Turns a concept, course or material into a learning cycle where **the learner explains before the coach intervenes**. The core: the learner produces the first draft of an explanation, and that explanation reveals the gaps.

## When to use

- Designing a "teach me by explaining" coaching session (teach-back).
- Turning a course, material or video into a Feynman cycle.
- Practicing learning by explaining on a concept.

## When not to use

- When the Feynman method is itself the subject of the course (meta-learning) → use a learning-strategies skill instead.
- For cloning the style of a material or importing a presentation.

## Fundamental principles

1. **The learner talks, the coach intervenes after.** Before any explanation from you, collect the learner's. No initial explanation → no Feynman cycle.
2. **One gap at a time.** From the explanation, find the 1–2 most critical gaps — not the whole list of shortcomings.
3. **Question before answer.** Use the minimum number of questions to close the gap. Only after several questions without progress do you give a hint or a scenario.
4. **Understanding before memorization.** The explanation must be re-writable and transferable. The learner must be able to do without jargon, rephrase simply, and re-explain in a different context.
5. **Material is only there to confirm.** Provided materials validate the concept's boundaries and check facts — they are not a source to copy a title from.

## Three constraints to define upfront

Before planning, clarify (only if the request is not enough):

1. **The concept's boundary**: this round covers only such-and-such core of the concept, not everything.
2. **The audience**: who the explanation is for (total novice, a peer who knows some of the domain, an expert from another domain).
3. **The teacher's constraints**: target depth, and what is allowed or not if the AI must answer live.

## The Feynman cycle (7 to 9 steps)

The cycle takes 7–9 turns. Do not skip a step; each link in the chain does not hold without the previous one.

### 1. The learner explains (teach-back)

- Briefly explain the concept's boundary and the goal, without giving a definition.
- Ask the learner not to use any material, to complete it in a single explanation.
- Prefer the learner's text or voice. If a component cannot keep its written trace, ask the learner to write or say their version, and let a teacher re-read it later to continue.

### 2. Find the smallest gap

- Decompose the initial explanation into core concepts to retain.
- Locate the 1–2 most critical jumps, abusive simplifications, reasoning errors or omissions.
- Have the learner estimate "I think it's there" — do not just give your diagnosis.

Note this diagnosis in your internal assessment, and turn it into a question the learner can ask themselves — do not present "gap 1", "gap 2" as internal headings.

### 3. Close the gap with questions

- One question at a time, on an already-defined point.
- Socratic questions centered on "why" and "if X changed, what would happen?".
- Do not give the answer until the learner has stumbled several times. A hint or a scenario is preferable to a direct answer.

### 4. Do a second pass

- Give a structuring instruction, ask the learner to produce two versions of the explanation.
- Insist on what must be marked as acquired, and on the points to keep for the written trace.
- Leave a written trace of version 1 and version 2, with the difference between the two.

### 5. Hunt the jargon

- From versions 1 and 2, pick the real jargon used.
- Ask the learner not to re-explain those words again, or give a counter-example and a risk of misunderstanding.
- Do not pre-fill a section with unrelated jargon beyond what the learner expresses.

### 6. Deconstruct their own analogies

- Ask the learner to propose an analogy, then to say "where it holds" and "where it breaks".
- At least one point of friction must emerge.
- The section does not pre-fill all failures; it is the learner who discovers them.

### 7. Transfer to a new context

- Have the concept transferred to a context where it has not been seen.
- Restrict the use of the concept's name: the learner must manage on the structure, not on the name.
- Add if possible a counter-example, a boundary or a change of condition, and verify the explanation still holds.

### 8. Complete the Feynman record

Final record, in the learner's words, fitting in a few lines:

1. What I can explain now;
2. How I learned it;
3. What I thought I knew;
4. My new explanation;
5. My analogy and its point of friction;
6. What I can explain on a new problem;
7. What I cannot explain yet, and the next target.

This is not "you learned well": it is the next cycle beginning.

## Roles (who does what)

- **The teacher / coach**: asks the questions after the teach-back, decides when to give a hint, makes responses be rephrased.
- **The novice / peer**: carries the dialogue, real or simulated, asks the questions, does not guess the answers.
- **The optional expert**: spots a wrong explanation, helps the learner identify it. Does not do the explanation in their place.

## Adaptation in a chat session

If the medium does not allow each step to run in real time (no interactive component), adapt:

1. **Teach-back**: collect the explanation, support self-verification with checkpoints.
2. **Roles**: the coach and the novice carry the dialogue, real or simulated; the coach asks the questions, does not give the answers.
3. **Written trace**: the coach rephrases what was understood and what remains, at the end of the turn, using the sections as a thread.

Do not turn a static or pre-generated section into a summative evaluation. Do not rely on a judgment about the text.

## Verification before finishing

- The initial explanation appeared **before** any content exposure;
- Each section says what it externalizes, what it retains, and what it rebuilds;
- Questions only target 1–2 gaps at a time;
- No direct answer was given before the learner stumbled;
- Jargon was hunted at least once;
- An analogy was stress-tested at least once;
- A transfer was produced in an unseen context;
- The final Feynman record exists and will serve as the starting point of the next cycle.
